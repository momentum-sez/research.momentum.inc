Continual learning across sovereign institutions

An essay on the institutional world model

21 September 2025 · Raeez Lorgat

Abstract. An institution often needs what another has already learned. It may not see the other’s cases. The lesson can prove wrong after requests have been decided under it. A later change of model can also stop a payment the institution already owes. We introduce Mass, a decentralized network of sovereign execution environments for AI agents. Each environment learns the correct, safe interpretation of its sovereign law, policy and regulation, and so keeps pace with fraud and bad actors on its own. Where its cases are too few, an environment can take what another has learned, its institutional world model, with a zero-knowledge proof that the model was computed as declared from cases the receiver never sees. The receiver decides how far to rely on the model and stops relying on it as soon as it records a correction to a case behind it. We give conditions under which a payment already owed is still completed as models change. In a worked example, checking a model from elsewhere against the receiver’s cases removes the harm of using it unchanged and leaves the receiver’s own result. For a receiver with no finding of its own, a second example shows what the model catches where a fraud scheme carries over and what it costs where it does not.

What an institution has to learn

Institutions learn slowly how their rules apply to new cases, usually after something has gone wrong. With AI agents sending requests without pause, they have to learn fast. Often one institution has already learned what another needs to know, since a scheme that works against one will be tried on the next. But the first institution’s case files are private. The second answers under its own law for every decision and cannot take a conclusion on trust, because what marks fraud in one place can be ordinary business in another. Institutions already share what they learn. They circulate descriptions of fraud methods and pool records, and some train a model jointly while each keeps its data. What arrives by any of these routes is information or a model. The receiver must trust that the cases behind it were as described and that the conclusion follows from them.

We introduce Mass, a decentralized network of sovereign execution environments for AI agents. Each institution runs one, a sovereign virtual machine, which holds its records and carries out the requests agents send it. The machine changes a record only by the institution’s rules and checks each requested change against them before making it. It is governed by the institution alone, under its law. Each environment learns the correct, safe interpretation of its sovereign law, policy and regulation, and so keeps pace with fraud and bad actors on its own. An interpretation is correct when it stays within the rule as those entitled to rule on it have read it, and safe to act on when the evidence and authority behind it are current and every other check on the act has passed. Where its cases are too few, an environment can also take what another has learned, with a proof that it follows from cases it never sees.

What an environment has learned is held in its institutional world model, a model of the institution’s rules, its procedures and the behavior they allow. The machine fits the model to its record of each request and refits it as the record grows. The record holds what was decided, under which rule and procedure, and what came of it.

A purchase of a company

Take one example. Carol is buying a company from Alice, its majority shareholder, for 100 units of currency. A registrar, the public office that keeps the register of who owns each company, must enter Carol in place of Alice. Carol’s bank, where she has placed the 100 in escrow, must pay Alice from it. Carol has authorized an AI agent to complete the purchase.

The agent sends its request to the registrar’s sovereign virtual machine. Before the machine changes the register, it checks that the agent holds Carol’s current authority, that the change requested is exactly the entry of Carol in place of Alice, and that every condition the registrar’s rules attach to a transfer is met. Most of these checks compare the request with a record. One depends on how a rule has been read. A transfer between related parties can be an ordinary reorganization, or it can be made at the expense of the company’s other shareholders. The registrar’s rule therefore says that such a transfer may need the consent of the minority shareholders. It does not say which relationships between buyer and seller call for it. That is settled in rulings. A ruling is the decision of one of a registrar’s officers on how its rule applies to one transfer, and it stands unless a court overturns it on appeal. The authority that records who controls companies has found that one investor, having financed both Carol and Alice, controls what each buys and sells. Buyer and seller are under common control. The registrar has never ruled on such a transfer, so its rulings cannot say whether consent is needed (Figure 1).

A model from another registrar

A registrar in another jurisdiction, the source of the model in this example, applies a rule of the same kind and has ruled on twenty-four transfers under it. The source sorts transfers by how buyer and seller are related. They are unrelated in an ordinary transfer. One party stands behind both in a transfer under common control, as with Carol and Alice. In a nominee or custody transfer one of them holds the shares on another’s behalf. The source has ruled on eight transfers of each kind.

For each kind the source computes a score by a counting rule. It adds one to the number of rulings that required consent and two to the number of rulings, then divides the first by the second. Seven of its eight rulings on common control required consent, which gives that kind a score of (7+1)/(8+2) = 4/5.

The procedure of Carol’s registrar requires the minority’s consent at a score of 3/4 or more and nothing further at 1/4 or less. In between, the rulings have left the question open and the case goes to an officer. So does any kind with no rulings at all, since the counting rule scores it at exactly 1/2.

Kind of transfer Rulings requiring consent, of eight Score Under the registrar’s procedure
Ordinary 0 1/10 No consent needed
Common control 7 4/5 Consent required
Nominee or custody 4 1/2 An officer decides

These three scores are an institutional world model at its smallest. They hold one thing learned about one rule, how it applies to three kinds of case. On these scores Carol’s purchase would have to wait until the minority shareholders consent, but the rulings behind the scores are the source’s private case files. Carol’s registrar may not see them, and it is being asked to delay her on the strength of three numbers it cannot check.

A proof in place of the cases

Cryptography lets the source show how the numbers were computed without showing the cases. The source publishes a signed list of its twenty-four cases. For each case the list gives an identifier and whether and when a ruling was recorded. It carries commitments to the two things it hides, the kind of transfer and the ruling. A commitment is a value computed from data that hides the data and fixes it, so that its author cannot later claim it stood for anything else.

The source then publishes the three scores with a zero-knowledge proof that they are exactly what the counting rule gives on the committed cases. A proof of this kind shows that a stated calculation was carried out correctly on hidden inputs. It reveals nothing about the inputs beyond what the result itself reveals. This one covers the whole list, so the source cannot count some of its listed cases and pass over others.

The registrar’s machine verifies the proof. If signatures cannot be forged, commitments cannot be reopened to other data and the proof system is sound, the scores follow from the committed cases by the counting rule.

In this example anyone who has the three scores and the length of the list can work out how many transfers of each kind the source ruled on and how many required consent. What stays hidden is which case is which. So two registrars can make this exchange even where neither has a duty to report to the other. Each still sends the facts that the law requires to the regulator it names.

The source needs the right to release a model, from its powers or from the agreement of those the cases concern. In the example it has that right. For everything else about the cases the registrar has the source’s word, that the source was entitled to rule, that its list is complete and that each case was sorted and recorded faithfully.

The registrar’s own decision

Before it acts on scores that rest on another registrar’s word, the registrar can look at what the source has published (Figure 2). The source publishes its rule and its definitions of the three kinds with the model, and the registrar compares them with its own. In the example they are the same. Even with matching rules, another institution’s result has effect in a jurisdiction only if the institution there gives it effect, for a purpose, under its rules or a treaty. The network paper, Sovereign economies on a decentralized network, calls this selective recognition. The registrar’s rules let it rely on the source’s rulings as grounds for requiring a consent.

The registrar still does not know whether the model holds for its cases. An institution with enough cases checks the model against them and, where the two disagree, uses the scores its cases give. The registrar has no ruling of its own on common control to set against the source’s score. It is in the position of an institution with no cases of the kind, which most needs the model and has the least to check it against. Such an institution can still try the model on the business it does know and reject one that would flag much of what it knows to be legitimate. Beyond that it can only limit what the model may do.

The registrar’s limit is set in an authorization that its director gives in advance. It says what a model may do and which models the machine may adopt without a new decision. In this case the director puts the source’s model on trial. A score may only hold a transfer while a consent is sought, and each such hold has a deadline. The machine may adopt one model, because many small revisions can add up to a change nobody authorized. On these terms the machine adopts the source’s model, assesses Carol’s request at 4/5 and holds it. Her agent sets about obtaining the minority’s consent.

A wrong model

Then a court in the source’s jurisdiction, on appeal, reverses six of the seven rulings that had required consent under common control.

The registrar’s machine has been relying on the reversed rulings. Each reversal reaches the registrar as a signed correction naming the case it corrects. Until that notice arrives, nothing the registrar can check will show the reversal, unless the source has undertaken to confirm on request that a ruling still stands. The machine checks the signature and records the correction. From that moment nothing that rested on the corrected rulings may be used in a further decision. The machine can find all of it in the record from which it learns, because with each assessment it kept the model that was used, and with the model the list it was fitted to.

The evidence behind the source’s model is out of date and acting on it is no longer safe. Carol’s hold loses its ground, because the registrar may not require the consent on the old score, and it may not register her transfer either until her request has been assessed again. No corrected model exists yet.

The source refits its model. With one ruling in eight requiring consent, common control scores (1+1)/(8+2) = 1/5. The source publishes the new scores with a new proof, which the machine verifies (Figure 3). The director’s authorization allowed one adoption and the first model used it, so the director has to renew it. Carol’s request waits for that decision under its original deadline. The machine then adopts the corrected model and assesses her request again. At 1/5 no consent is needed.

Carol has waited for a consent she did not need. The correction does not undo that, and who compensates her is for a court. The machine keeps what a court would need to decide it, a record of who ruled, who relied on the ruling and how long she was held.

A payment still owed

The registrar and the bank now decide the purchase together, each checking its part under its rules, by the protocol that the network paper calls polycentric consensus. Either Carol is entered in place of Alice and the bank is instructed to pay Alice from the escrow, or neither happens. When the two commit, Carol is on the register and the machine holds on its record an instruction to the bank to pay Alice 100. Alice has given up her shares and holds only a right to be paid her 100 until the bank pays. The machine has still to release the instruction to the bank and, when the bank reports that it has paid, to match the report to the instruction and record the payment as made. Before the bank pays, one more correction arrives from the source, a seventh.

The machine records it. After the first six, Carol’s request could not go forward until it had been assessed again. This one finds the purchase decided and leaves the instruction to pay Alice untouched. But the registrar’s models go on changing, and a payment already owed can be stopped by a stricter check adopted after it was promised. A check on payments between parties financed by one investor would stop this one. The instruction would stay on the books and Alice would never be paid.

So before the machine adopts any model, it checks every duty still owed, Alice’s payment among them. A next step must remain that the new model permits. Whatever the bank replies to that step, the payment must be made or still able to be made, and never made twice. A model that fails this for Alice’s payment is not adopted while her payment is owed.

That check takes one model at a time. A series of models, each leaving a way forward, could still keep sending a payment back to an earlier step. Every duty therefore carries a count of the steps remaining, fixed when the duty is created. Alice’s payment counts two at the commit, one once the instruction is with the bank and zero when the payment is recorded. A completed step lowers the count and no model may raise it. A court can still order the payment stopped, on the record, but a stricter model that would add a step to a payment already owed, such as a review before release, is refused.

If at least one step is completed in every b changes the machine makes to its records, a duty with r steps remaining is complete within b \times r changes, and Alice’s within 2b (Figure 4). If the bank never acts, the payment stays on the record, owed and unpaid. In the example the bank pays. The machine matches the bank’s report to the instruction and records the payment as made in full.

What has been shown

The purchase proceeds in order from Carol’s request to the bank’s payment (Figure 5).

The results the story relied on are proved in the paper this essay accompanies, The institutional world model: Continual learning across sovereign virtual machines. The construction that proves the scores covers three kinds of case and lists of up to 256. A duty that passes the check before adoption can be completed under the new model and, with a count of steps, within the bound above. Nothing that rested on a corrected ruling supports a further decision until it has a new ground, and no model removes from the record an instruction that can still be carried out. The results suppose that every change to the records passes the machine’s checks and is made whole or not at all, and that sources and the bank keep to what they have declared.

Whether another’s model is worth having is a separate question. The first example takes a receiver with cases of its own and asks what checking the model against them is worth. Here the scores are open and carry no proofs. At a source, 16 of the 20 cases that followed one commercial practice were fraud, a score of 17/22. At the receiver the practice is ordinary business. None of the 40 cases it kept for checking was fraud, a score of 1/42. The receiver faces 320 new requests, 26 fraudulent and 294 legitimate, and holds for investigation any that scores 1/2 or more. A model learned from the receiver’s cases holds 24 of the 26 frauds and 16 legitimate requests. The source’s model used unchanged holds the same 24 frauds and 96 legitimate requests. The extra 80 all follow the practice. Checked against the receiver’s cases, the source’s model ends with the receiver’s scores and the receiver’s result, so the check removes the harm of using it as it came.

The second example takes the case in which another’s model is needed most, a receiver with no finding of its own. A source learns a fraud scheme from its findings, as a rule of six conditions on twelve yes-or-no facts about a request. It releases the model, with how often each pattern of the twelve facts occurs with each finding, and a proof of correct fitting over 6,000 cases. The receiver verifies the proof, checks the model against its cases and the authorization it gave in advance, and uses the model on trial from the first day. The check rejects a model fitted to wrong findings and one that would sweep in legitimate local business, because each would flag much of what the receiver knows to be legitimate. A model poisoned on purpose passes, since the receiver has no finding of the scheme to test it against. If the model is correct and the scheme works the same way at the receiver, the receiver holds every instance of the scheme in the week before its first finding. It also holds about one legitimate request in 64, which it would have passed with no model. If the scheme never arrives or works differently, the receiver holds the same one in 64 and catches nothing beyond chance.

The consent scores are learned from the outcomes of rulings. An officer’s decision is a new ruling, and the next fitting counts its outcome: a ninth nominee ruling that required consent would move the nominee score from 1/2 to 6/11.
