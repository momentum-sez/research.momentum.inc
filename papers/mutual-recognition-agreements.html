Mutual Recognition

Raeez Lorgat, 2026

When an entity enters an additional jurisdiction, the destination evaluates its compliance against local rules. The corridor is the channel from a jurisdiction where the entity already operates to the one it is entering. Its re-evaluation set records the compliance domains the destination evaluates locally instead of accepting the entity’s existing evaluations. How small can this set be in principle?

The redundancy problem

Much of the re-evaluation is redundant because regulatory requirements derive from common standards. Anti-money-laundering (AML) regimes implement the Financial Action Task Force (FATF) Recommendations, with the same know-your-customer duties and suspicious-transaction reporting. Bank capital rules implement the Basel framework, and data-privacy statutes in many jurisdictions track the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation. Where two jurisdictions implement the same standard and enforce it comparably, a second full evaluation adds cost without adding regulatory information. Where Pakistan and Singapore both build their AML regimes on the FATF Recommendations and hold comparable assessments, a second full AML evaluation repeats the first. Where the UAE and Bahrain impose materially equivalent data-privacy requirements, re-evaluation is duplicative.

The solution is mutual recognition: jurisdiction A accepts jurisdiction B’s evaluations in stated domains, and B accepts A’s. An entity that passes AML evaluation in A uses that evaluation on entering B, where it is accepted without re-evaluation.

Recognition per domain and under stated conditions is established practice. A product lawfully marketed in one EU member state circulates in the others. The 1998 EU–US Mutual Recognition Agreement applies the principle to conformity assessment, and professional-licensing reciprocity applies it to qualifications.

The EU’s equivalence decisions recognize a third country’s regime through separate legal acts, subject to conditions, revocation, and sometimes a stated period. Under Article 25 of the European Market Infrastructure Regulation, a non-EU clearing house requires a Commission decision that its home framework is equivalent before recognition. Equivalence for Swiss stock exchanges concerned the obligation to trade shares on recognized venues. It was limited from the start to the end of 2018, extended once to 30 June 2019, and not renewed.

United States substituted compliance likewise recognizes per requirement and on conditions. Foreign swap dealers satisfy stated Dodd-Frank requirements through compliance with a home regime that the Commodity Futures Trading Commission has found comparable. Since December 2013, it has made such findings for Australia, Canada, the EU, Hong Kong, Japan, and Switzerland. Existing practice supplies neither a common vocabulary of grades across domains and instruments nor recognized terms in a form a system can execute.

Binary recognition is too coarse

Binary recognition either accepts or rejects a jurisdiction’s evaluations, leaving no grade for acceptance subject to review.

Consider three jurisdictions evaluating AML compliance. Jurisdiction A has a strong, well-enforced regime. Jurisdiction B has the rules without the enforcement: compliant in letter, weak in operation. Jurisdiction C has fundamental deficiencies. Binary recognition can distinguish acceptance from rejection, but it has no intermediate grade for acceptance subject to review. Graded recognition accepts A’s evaluations, accepts B’s subject to review, and declines C’s.

The FATF’s assessment methodology helps calibrate these grades. FATF rates each jurisdiction’s AML framework on two scales: technical compliance, per Recommendation, from Compliant down to Non-Compliant, and effectiveness, per outcome, from High down to Low. The scales can diverge, as in the distinction between A and B: a framework compliant in letter can remain weak in operation. Both scales rate national frameworks rather than entities. A FATF mutual evaluation grades the regime under which entities are evaluated and recognizes no entity’s compliance.

A pair of favorable ratings therefore provides evidence for a corridor’s recognition grade, never a portable pass for an entity. Recognition transports the entity-level evaluation performed under the rated framework. The framework’s rating calibrates how far the destination trusts that evaluation.

A mutual recognition agreement should express that calibration directly, per domain:

  • Full recognition. The foreign evaluation is accepted without local re-evaluation. Warranted where the source regime is strong in letter and in operation.

  • Partial recognition. The foreign evaluation is accepted and flagged for local review. The natural grade where a regime is compliant in letter and uneven in operation.

  • Conditional recognition. The foreign evaluation is accepted only while a stated condition holds. The condition may bind the source jurisdiction’s standing — FATF membership in good standing, a treaty in force — or the particular evaluation, such as enhanced due diligence performed. While the condition fails, the grade behaves as no recognition.

  • No recognition. Full local re-evaluation. The foreign evaluation carries no reliable signal.

Recognition is assigned per domain. A jurisdiction may merit full recognition on AML and partial recognition on data privacy. Its weak data-privacy regime says nothing about its AML evaluations, and its strong AML regime gives no assurance about its data-privacy evaluations.

The sanctions exception

Sanctions is excluded from cross-sovereign recognition by default. A foreign sanctions clearance supports local clearance only under a condition on the lists themselves. Ordinary recognition does not establish that condition.

Recognition transports assessments of the entity. An AML evaluation asks whether the entity’s controls meet a standard, and a data-privacy evaluation asks whether its handling of personal data meets one. These are facts about the entity, and where two standards are materially equivalent, one jurisdiction’s finding answers the other’s question.

Other domains concern statuses indexed to a single jurisdiction: registration of an offering, remittance of withholding, customs clearance, or authorization of a currency transfer. Registration in A is not registration in B, and no equivalence of standards makes it so. Such statuses cross borders only under instruments that create the local status by law — passporting regimes, treaty credit for tax paid, customs unions — never by one jurisdiction accepting another’s finding. Recognition applies to the entity-intrinsic domains. Jurisdiction-indexed statuses remain local unless their own legal instruments provide otherwise.

A sanctions clearance in A asserts that the entity appears on none of A’s lists. It is a membership test against A’s own designations, rather than an assessment of the entity. The destination tests whether the entity appears on B’s lists. This tests B’s designations, and A’s answer settles it exactly when every designation B applies is one A applies too. An entity on none of A’s lists is then on none of B’s.

Otherwise A’s clearance leaves B’s question open. A corridor that accepted it as B’s would wrongly clear precisely the entities B has designated and A has not.

Extraterritorial regimes add consequences. The United States regime reaches in practice the entire dollar-clearing system because dollar payments clear through United States banks. 50 U.S.C. § 1702(a)(1), section 203(a)(1) of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, gives the President authority to regulate or prohibit certain bank transfers. It covers transfers of credit or payments through any such bank where a foreign interest is involved.

Secondary sanctions rest on further authorities, including the Countering America’s Adversaries Through Sanctions Act and program-specific executive orders. These reach non-United States persons who deal with designated ones. A corridor that inherited clearance could carry forward a permissive verdict the United States does not recognize. This exposes the entity’s dollar transactions to prohibition under the first authority and the entity itself to designation under the second.

The condition concerns how the lists are constituted. It holds reliably only by construction: every list B applies is a list A applies because both adopt it from the authority that sets it. Like the corridor, the condition is directed. Suppose B applies the United Nations Security Council’s consolidated list alone and A applies that list with national additions. Then A’s clearance settles B’s question, but B’s clearance does not settle A’s: B never screened the national designations.

Shared authority is the symmetric case: both sides apply exactly the shared lists and each direction inherits clearance. Security Council designations under Chapter VII bind every member state, and centrally decided EU sanctions bind every member state identically. Within such a subnetwork, the corridor inherits the single test against the shared lists. A member that adds national designations screens those itself, and sanctions leaves its re-evaluation set only where it adds none. The intra-EU case therefore falls within shared authority. It does not involve recognition between distinct sanctions sovereigns.

Sanctions leaves a corridor’s re-evaluation set only under an explicit shared-authority certificate. This signed artifact certifies, for the corridor’s direction, that the destination applies no list the source does not. It names the common authority or authorities, the covered lists or decision process, the bound parties, the validity window, and the revocation procedure. Every corridor outside such a certificate — every general-purpose corridor — re-evaluates sanctions locally, whatever else it recognizes, and so retains at least one domain evaluation.

Counting what recognition removes

Count domain evaluations for six jurisdictions forming two federations of three. Each pair defines a corridor, giving fifteen corridors, six choose two. Each direction carries its own terms, but for this count let both directions have the same terms and count each pair once. Counting directions separately doubles every figure and changes no ratio.

Suppose each cross-border corridor re-evaluates six domains: AML, sanctions, foreign-exchange controls, customs, tax withholding, and data privacy. Each intra-federal corridor re-evaluates three: AML, tax withholding, and data privacy. Sanctions, customs, and exchange controls drop out because a single federal authority sets them for both sides. For sanctions, this is the shared-authority case.

The baseline across the network is the sum of domain evaluations over corridors: nine cross-border corridors at six each and six intra-federal corridors at three each, seventy-two evaluations.

Now introduce a four-party agreement at full recognition covering AML and data privacy — two entity-intrinsic domains present in every corridor’s baseline. The agreement reaches the six corridors among its parties, four choose two, and removes two evaluations from each. It removes twelve of the seventy-two evaluations, one sixth of the network total, by covering two domains. In general, a k-party agreement at full recognition over a domain set D removes |D| \cdot k(k-1)/2 evaluations, provided each covered domain lies in each affected corridor’s baseline.

These exact counts measure evaluations. They measure cost only under an assumption of equal evaluation costs, which does not hold: a sanctions list screen is a lookup, and an AML controls assessment is an audit. Economic comparisons must weight the domains by their evaluation costs.

The reduction grows with membership. When a fifth jurisdiction accedes, four new corridors come under the agreement at once: the (k{+}1)-th accession creates k recognized corridors, so the benefit of acceding grows linearly with membership. The cost of acceding depends on the instrument — negotiation with each member pair by pair, or a single act where the agreement carries an accession clause admitting new parties on its standard terms. An accession clause keeps the cost of joining from growing with membership. Recognition alone does not.

The floor persists under any expansion. Recognition reaches the entity-intrinsic domains. Jurisdiction-indexed domains leave a baseline only under their own instruments: a customs union, an exchange-control exemption, or treaty treatment of withholding. A corridor whose parties have all of those still screens sanctions locally. Recognition and union-type instruments together can reduce a cross-sovereign corridor’s baseline to a single evaluation. Reaching zero requires a certificate that the destination applies no sanctions list the source does not. Shared authority is the symmetric case.

The naming problem

Jurisdictions name the same regulatory concept differently. One’s “securities regulation” is another’s “capital markets oversight”, and one’s “foreign-exchange controls” are another’s “currency transfer restrictions.” The same name can also span different substance: one jurisdiction’s “AML” covers money laundering alone, while another’s spans counter-terrorism financing as well.

Matching compliance domains by name can therefore fail in two ways. Different names for the same substance prevent the destination from finding a source evaluation to accept, causing redundant work. The same name for different substance can clear components the source never evaluated. For example, the broader jurisdiction’s counter-terrorism-financing requirement would stand satisfied by an evaluation that never examined it.

A domain map agreed at corridor formation translates between the two regulatory vocabularies and forms part of the corridor’s definition. The map is partial: it has an entry only where the corridor instrument certifies that the source evaluation covers the destination domain’s substance. A domain without a certified counterpart is not mapped, so a missing entry causes redundant evaluation rather than a missed requirement. Where coverage is partial, the corridor declines the mapping or conditions recognition on local evaluation of the remainder.

The map also bounds what recognition can do. An agreement shrinks the re-evaluation set only over domains the map connects. Full recognition of AML has no effect until the corridor records which destination domain it reaches.

Recognition as a computable object

Mutual recognition operates through treaties and equivalence decisions, regulations and supervisory guidance. Compliance staff apply these instruments, and regulatory updates determine when recognition changes. When entities cross dozens of corridors daily and every crossing must produce a re-evaluation set, the operative content of the agreement has to be queryable at machine speed.

A computable representation records the treaty’s operative provisions: its parties, recognized domains, grades, and conditions. A system can query it (“does the agreement between A and B cover domain d, and how?”) and adjust the corridor’s re-evaluation set mechanically.

The computable object supplements the treaty, which retains its preambles, definitions, dispute-resolution procedures, and sunset clauses. It represents the part every crossing consults: which domains, at what grade, under which conditions.

A grade per domain makes each recognition decision explicit: whether AML is fully recognized, and whether data privacy is conditionally recognized under a stated condition. This removes ambiguity about the grade and condition during negotiation, when the parties can address gaps that an informal arrangement leaves until the border.

The shape of the solution

A corridor from jurisdiction A to jurisdiction B is a directed triple (R, \mu, \gamma). Its re-evaluation set R contains the destination-side domains that B re-evaluates locally. The partial domain map \mu translates A’s domain names to B’s only where substantive coverage is certified. It is one-to-one: each recognized source domain reaches one destination domain, and each destination domain receives at most one source domain. Per-domain grade maps \gamma state how B consumes recognized evaluations: at full strength, flagged for review, or under conditions. These maps never exceed source strength, so recognition cannot strengthen a foreign verdict in transit.

The one-to-one form makes each grade map run from one source coordinate to one destination coordinate. This restriction can require redundant evaluation. Where one source evaluation certifiably covers several destination domains — a broad AML evaluation that examined terrorism financing covers a destination’s separate money-laundering and terrorism-financing domains — the instrument records one of them at formation and the rest fall to local re-evaluation: redundant work, never a missed requirement.

The maps are partial and fail closed: a destination domain \mu does not reach, and a source grade \gamma does not define, both fall to local re-evaluation. Corridors are directed because recognition is directed: A’s acceptance of B’s AML evaluations does not imply B’s acceptance of A’s, and each direction carries its own triple.

A mutual recognition agreement is an instrument that instantiates (\mu, \gamma) and shrinks R. Several agreements may apply to one corridor, and they compose by a fixed rule. The map is one recorded object: where applicable agreements certify different source domains for the same destination domain, the corridor records one entry at formation, and only grades attached to the recorded source evaluation count.

Grades compose by taking the strongest. At a crossing, each applicable grade resolves: a conditional grade becomes full while its condition holds and none while it fails. Then B consumes the strongest resolved grade under the order none, partial, full. Each agreement separately commits B to accept the evaluation on its terms, so the entity crosses on the most favorable one. A destination domain leaves R when its strongest resolved grade is above none.

Two properties follow from the definitions. Applying an agreement never enlarges R: a further agreement adds a ground for acceptance, and the strongest of more grounds is never weaker. Partiality is safe because undefined inputs trigger local re-evaluation. An incomplete map causes redundant work, never a missed requirement.

Sanctions is the standing exception. It leaves R only under an explicit shared-authority certificate, never by ordinary recognition, because a foreign clearance answers a question about foreign lists, and answers the local question only where the local lists are among them by construction.

The re-evaluation set identifies the domains B evaluates afresh: those that remain in R after every applicable agreement is applied. The work that remains is that local evaluation together with the obligations \gamma attaches to what it recognizes — the local review that partial recognition carries and the condition monitoring that conditional recognition carries.

Under fixed corridor parameters R is deterministic: the map is one recorded object, and taking the strongest grade is independent of agreement order. Recognition never enlarges it. Without a shared-authority certificate over sanctions lists, it contains at least one domain: the local sanctions evaluation. The grades B consumes, and the obligations they carry, are determined with R: each is the strongest resolved grade for its domain under the same parameters.
